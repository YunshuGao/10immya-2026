DO NOW Pre-test (no wrong answers)

Don't mark these. They're a baseline. Skim them to spot misconceptions you'll fix during the lesson.

Q1 When you take a photo with your phone in a dark room and it comes out grainy and dark, what is the camera struggling with?
No marks — pre-test only
What they should sense
Light. The sensor doesn't have enough light to record a clean image, so the phone amplifies the signal (creating noise = the grain).
Common answers: "not enough light", "the camera can't see", "grainy because dark" — all fine. They'll learn the proper term (exposure) in Part 4.
Q2 Why do photographers use bigger, heavier cameras (DSLRs) instead of just their phones?
No marks — pre-test only
What they should sense
Bigger sensors, interchangeable lenses, manual control of shutter/aperture/ISO, more light gathered = better picture in tricky conditions.
Common (fine): "better quality", "more zoom", "looks more professional". Leave it — Part 2 + 3 will validate this.
Q3 What does “photograph” actually mean? Break the word apart.
Hint given: "two Greek words"
Model answer
Photo = light · graph = writing → "light writing" or "writing with light".
Watch: "photo = picture" — wrong etymology. Gently correct in class.

PART 1 Camera & Light (Q4–Q6) · ~3 marks

Q4 What did “camera” originally mean? (Multiple choice)easy · /1
Recall — direct from video
Model answer
B — A room (from Latin camera obscura = dark room).
Distractor "A box" is tempting — they may pick it because cameras look like boxes today. Mark wrong.
Q5 Why do we use lenses instead of pinholes? Main reason. easy · /1
Model answer
Pinholes let through very little light, so exposures take far too long. Lenses gather much more light and focus it onto the sensor — letting us take pictures in a fraction of a second.
Look for: mention of more light OR faster exposure. Either is enough for full mark.
Wrong: "lenses make it sharper" — partly true but misses the real reason (light).
Q6 The image on the sensor is upside-down. Why doesn't this matter to you when filming? easy · /1
Model answer
The camera flips the image right-side-up before showing it on the screen / saving the file. We never see the upside-down version.
Accept: "the camera flips it", "the software/computer corrects it", "I only see the final image". All correct.

PART 2 Focal Length & Framing (Q7–Q10) · ~6 marks

Q7 Match focal length → angle of view (dropdown table). medium · /4
Direct from Peter's diagram at 9:00
Model answers (all four)
• 15 mm = about 180° (super wide)
• 35 mm = about 60° (standard)
• 70 mm = about 34° (light telephoto)
• 200 mm = about 12° (heavy telephoto)
Marking: 1 mark per correct row. The relationship — smaller mm = wider angle — is the real concept; if they get the pattern but mix up two numbers, give half marks.
Q8 Wide establishing shot of the quad — short or long focal length? easy · /1
Model answer
Short (wide angle, ~15–35 mm) — to fit the whole quad in frame.
Q9 Teacher's face across the room, blurry background — short or long? medium · /1
This bridges to Part 4 (aperture/depth of field)
Model answer
Long (telephoto, ~100–200 mm) — telephoto lenses compress depth, making backgrounds blur more easily.
Watch for: students picking "short" because they think wide = closer to the subject. Wide does NOT mean close-up.
Q10 Why does the same 50 mm lens look "normal" on one camera but "telephoto" on a smaller one? stretch · /2
Crop factor — the trickiest concept in Video 1
Model answer
A smaller sensor only captures the middle part of the image the lens projects, so it looks like a zoom (telephoto). The lens didn't change — the sensor cropped what it could see. (This is the "crop factor".)
Full marks: mentions sensor size AND cropping/zoom effect.
Half marks: says "because of the sensor" without the cropping logic.

PART 3 Sensor in 60 seconds (Q11–Q12) · ~2 marks

Q11 In one sentence: what does a sensor do? easy · /1
Model answer
A sensor converts light into electrical signals that the camera/computer reads as a picture.
Accept: "turns light into data/electricity", "records the light", "captures light as info".
Half mark: "takes a picture" (too vague).
Q12 Three standard sensor sizes (largest to smallest). easy · /1
Model answer
1. Full Frame (36 × 24 mm) → 2. APS-C (27 × 23 mm) → 3. Four Thirds (21 × 17 mm)
Accept: just the names (Full Frame · APS-C · Four Thirds) in correct order. Dimensions optional.
Wrong order = half mark.

EXTENSION Optional bonus (EX1–EX2)

Only mark if attempted. Don't penalise students who skipped.

EX1 Why is the Bayer Array twice as much green as red or blue? stretch · bonus
Model answer
Because the human eye is most sensitive to green light — we see more detail in greens. The sensor is designed to mimic our eye, so the extra green pixels give it the same colour bias we have.
EX2 What does the second curtain of a focal-plane shutter do? stretch · bonus
Model answer
It chases the first curtain to cover the sensor again and end the exposure. The gap between the two curtains controls how long each pixel is exposed — that's the shutter speed.
Half mark: mentions "closes" or "ends exposure" without explaining the gap.

PART 4 Exposure Triangle (Q13–Q16) · ~5 marks

Q13 1/125 → 1/250 sec: more, less or same light? easy · /1
Model answer
Half as much light. Faster shutter = sensor exposed for half the time. Each stop on the shutter-speed scale doubles or halves the light.
Common error: "more light because the number is bigger" — wrong, the number is a denominator (1/250 is shorter than 1/125).
Q14 Which lets more light: f/2.8 or f/16? medium · /1
The famous trick question
Model answer
f/2.8 — because it's a smaller number. Counter-intuitive: bigger f-number = smaller aperture hole = less light. (f-number = focal length ÷ aperture diameter.)
Very common error: "f/16 because bigger number". Mark wrong but explain the f-number maths in feedback.
Q15 f/4 at 1/60 (sharp) vs f/16 at ¼ (blurry) — same exposure, why is right one blurry? medium · /1
Model answer
Because the right photo had a much slower shutter speed (¼ sec vs 1/60). During that quarter-second, the flower or camera moved — creating motion blur. Same total light reached the sensor, but the shutter was open long enough to record movement.
Full mark: identifies slow shutter as cause.
Half: mentions aperture but not shutter (some get confused which is which).
Q16 "The easy part is getting right light. The hard part is choosing the COMBINATION that creates the picture you want." Explain. stretch · /2
This is the BIG IDEA — concept check
Model answer (in their own words)
Lots of shutter+aperture combinations give the same overall brightness, but each one looks different. Fast shutter + wide aperture = frozen, sharp, blurry background. Slow shutter + narrow aperture = motion blur, everything in focus. The photographer's job is picking the combo that matches the story they want to tell.
Full marks: mentions different combos can give same exposure but different look.
Half: only says "you have a choice" without explaining the trade-off.

APPLY IT To their group video (Q17–Q20) · ~4 marks

Personal answers — mark on sensible reasoning, not specific values.

Q17 Describe a shot from their Task 3 video. easy · /1
Look for
Specific: location, action, who's in it. (One concrete sentence is enough.)
If they wrote "an interview" — push back to add where and who.
Q18 Wide / normal / telephoto — and WHY? medium · /1
Look for
Choice matched to the shot:
• Wide quad/landscape → short focal length
• Two people talking → normal (~35–50 mm)
• Close emotion / blurry background → telephoto
Justification matters more than the specific value.
Q19 Bright or dim location, and what to change if dim. medium · /1
Look for
If dim, any ONE of:
• Slow the shutter (allows more light, but risks motion blur)
• Open the aperture (lower f-number)
• Raise ISO (more sensitive sensor)
• Add light (lamp, reflector, window)
Wrong: "use flash" alone — most phones can't run continuous flash for video, and direct flash looks ugly. Accept if they mention continuous light source.
Q20 Fast movement — freeze or blur? Which shutter speed? medium · /1
Look for
To freeze: fast shutter — 1/500 sec or faster.
To blur creatively: slow shutter — 1/30 or slower (with tripod).
Acceptable values vary; reasoning is the mark.
Bonus: mentions 180° shutter rule for video (1/double-the-frame-rate) — that's pro-level.
